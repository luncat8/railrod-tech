# 0.1.9 — the air the gauge pays for

Parent: `0.1.0-plan.md` (the knob contract), `0.1.8-plan.md` (the line and the
bench). Parked planner work: `deferred-plan.md`.

## The finding

Measured across the gauge slider, a line got **monotonically faster** the wider the
gauge was: 183 s at 0.6 m down to 143 s at 3.32 m, on the default build, seed
731421, two laps. Nothing in the model pushed back on that. The reason is that the
gauge had three channels and only one of them pointed at motion:

| channel | direction | on the wire |
|---|---|---|
| permitted speed `v_track` | rises with `g` | `V0 · (0.7 + 0.6g)` — an 86 % spread |
| hold per wagon | rises with `g` | `1 + (HOLD_MAX − 1) · g` |
| track, wagon and mass | rises with `g` | money and iron only |

and `F_res = c_rr·M·G + c_drag·v³` had **no gauge in it at all**. A train on 4.0 m
track pushed exactly as much air as one on 0.6 m. So the two things a narrow gauge
actually buys — less mass and less frontal area — were invisible: the train was
speed-capped on every build in the middle of the slider, with 0.34 km/s² of
acceleration spare at `g = 0` and nothing at all spare at `g = 1`. The engine knob
was dead at the narrow end and the mass knob was dead everywhere, and the only
brake on going wide was money.

Two consequences worth naming:

- **The gauge read as a ramp, not a trade-off.** One of the three channels the
  player watches (time) had no interior optimum, so the lesson the instrument
  taught was "wider is faster; stop when it gets expensive".
- **Line time was carrying a second claim it did not advertise.** A line ends at
  the first clean stop past the finish, so its clock is `distance / pace` *plus*
  whatever further the market made it run to find that stop. Measured on seed
  20240, four wagons: a build at `g = 0.6` ran 167.8 km and one at `g = 0.7` ran
  295.8 km. Both were called "slower" by the clock and only one of them was slower
  on the track.

## The change

**Air is paid for in cross-section.** The loading gauge *is* the vehicle's
cross-section, so `c_drag` stops being a flat coefficient and starts being a
coefficient per unit of frontal area:

```
area(g)   = gauge_m(g) / GAUGE_MAX_M      // 0.15 at 0.6 m, 1.0 at 4.0 m
F_drag    = C_DRAG · area(g) · v³         // C_DRAG 2.4 → 8.4
```

A train on 4.0 m track now pushes 6.7× the frontal area of one on 0.6 m. `C_DRAG`
is rescaled so the middle of the slider pays what it paid before; the ends move
apart by 3.8× (narrow) and 1.7× (wide).

**The track limit stops being a gift of the gauge.** The safe speed on a curve is
the gauge against the height the loading gauge lets the vehicle carry, and a narrow
train is narrow *and* low — the two scale together and largely cancel. What is left
is a track limit that is nearly flat across the slider, with a modest stability
bonus for width:

```
v_track   = V0 · (0.85 + 0.45g) · (0.9 + 0.2d)   // was (0.7 + 0.6g)
```

The widest gauge is allowed exactly what it was allowed before; only the narrow end
is lifted, by 21 %.

**The two cross in the middle.** Below the crossing a train reaches the speed it is
allowed and is held down by its track; above it a train cannot reach the speed it
is allowed and is held down by its own bulk. That is where the interior optimum
comes from, and it is emergent: it is not a term in any curve.

Measured, four wagons, wheel and engine at 0.5, one loaded lap of the ring from a
standing start with nothing to stop for:

```
g        0.0    0.2    0.4    0.5    0.6    0.8    1.0
allowed  1.360  1.504  1.648  1.720  1.792  1.936  2.080
reached  1.360  1.504  1.648  1.698  1.644  1.547  1.472
lap s    49.9   46.2   43.7   43.1   44.3   46.9   49.4
```

The peak is at 2.30 m and both ends are 15 % slower than it.

## The line's clock is not the line's pace

The meter keeps `move`, the seconds it spent under way, alongside `time`. `dwell`
is the difference, `pace` is `km / move`, and both are reported:

- the footer's `LINE TIME` row carries the pace beside the seconds —
  `146.3 S · 1.19 KM/S` — so a slow line and a slow build are never the same claim;
- a bench row's tooltip splits the clock the same way and names the distance run;
- a bench row carries `CR/S` beside its total, which is the unit a swept dot is
  measured in, so a row and a dot for the same build can be read against each other
  (the next step 0.1.8 left over).

## Checks

- `experiments/gauge.js` is new: the area spans the slider the way the gauge does
  and rises monotonically; at a speed the whole slider can reach a wider gauge
  accelerates worse; the narrowest build reaches the speed its track allows and the
  widest does not; the fastest lap and the fastest line pace are both strictly
  inside the slider with a margin at both ends; and a line's clock splits exactly
  into seconds at a stop and seconds under way.
- The 0.1.8 gate is unchanged: `line.js` still reports a headless row as an exact
  field-for-field replay of the live line, and `surface.js` still reports an
  interior single-peaked optimum on four of five seeds with the enormous build
  losing on every one.
- `experiments/tech.js` tracks the new `v_track` at `g = d = 0.5` (1.72 km/s);
  `experiments/train.js` no longer assumes a fixed 1.2 km/s is above the narrowest
  cap — it asks `Tech.vTrackOf` for it.

## Left over

- The overshoot is still not a knob: a line can run up to `LINE_CUTOFF_KM` before
  it is cut, and the report shows rows between 167.8 km and 384 km on a 128 km
  line. The pace takes the sting out of it, but a cap of one extra lap would make
  the totals comparable as well as legible.
- `area(g)` is linear in the gauge. A real cross-section is width × height, and
  both scale with the loading gauge, so `g²` is at least as defensible; the linear
  form was chosen because it keeps the middle of the slider near where it was.
- Skin friction belongs to the length of the train, not its face, and is not
  modelled: an eight-wagon narrow-gauge consist pays the same air as a one-wagon
  one. That is the channel that would make "many small wagons" cost what it should.
